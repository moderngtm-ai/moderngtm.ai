## Executive summary

AI is redefining go-to-market work, but the evidence of results is concentrated. The clearest reported gains come from companies that rebuilt a specific workflow around AI, measured it by the unit of work and redeployed the time it freed. Beyond those cases the record is mixed, not negative: BCG counts a further 41% of companies scaling AI and outperforming laggards, and a peer-reviewed field study of 5,172 support agents found that AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. The surveys that compare leaders with laggards point to operating practice more than access to tools, but they show association, not cause.

**Key findings**

1. **Almost every team uses AI; a minority say it is ready.** Both surveys behind this come from vendors. In Salesloft's 2026 benchmark of 500 U.S. revenue leaders at companies with 200 or more employees, every respondent uses AI somewhere in the revenue process, but 20.6% describe it as production-ready with measurable outcomes. In LeanData's customer survey of 157 GTM practitioners, 93% have deployed at least one AI agent, while 31% call their infrastructure ready and 63% somewhat ready.
2. **Where work has been rebuilt, companies report automation rates from above 30% to 91%, each on its own measure.** The reported rates run from more than 30% of client requests resolved without a person to 91% of support tickets automated (Figure 2). Because each company counts differently, the rates are not comparable.
3. **Leaders and laggards differ sharply, but the comparisons are associations.** In McKinsey's 2026 B2B survey, 60% of market-share leaders grew revenue by double digits, against 21% of laggards. BCG's 7.5% of “future-built” companies had 2.4 times the revenue growth and 2.8 times the EBITDA growth of laggards over 2022 to 2025, figures BCG takes from financial databases. Both studies group companies by performance or AI maturity, so neither shows that AI caused the gap.
4. **The leaders' common practice is redesign before automation.** Bain's top performers and BCG's future-built companies describe redesigning a workflow before automating it. In the peer-reviewed field study, less experienced support agents gained most from AI assistance, and its authors offer suggestive evidence that the tool spread the practices of more able agents. Building an agent from the best performer's process is a practice worth testing, not a proven cause of growth.
5. **More service leaders report moving people than cutting them, and entry-level employment is weakening.** In Gartner's 2026 survey of 275 service and support leaders, 21% had reduced agent headcount because of AI. A larger share, 71%, were shifting agents into different roles within service and support. Gartner's release ties only the headcount cut to AI. Stanford finds that employment of U.S. workers aged 22 to 25 in highly AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers.
6. **Cutting on cost alone risks quality, though the best-known case is disputed.** Klarna's CEO told Bloomberg in May 2025 that cost had weighed too heavily and quality had suffered, and Klarna began recruiting human agents. Klarna later told SEC staff that the remarks concerned a pilot of freelance customer experience consultants and that it runs AI and human support side by side. Its annual report for 2025 says the assistant handled 80% of customer service chats, with satisfaction on a par with human agents by Klarna's own surveys. Gartner predicts that half of the companies that cut service staff because of AI will rehire by 2027.
7. **Most sales organizations report low reinvestment of the time AI saves.** AI saves sellers 4.8 hours a week, yet 72% of sales organizations report low reinvestment of that time in high-value work (Gartner, 210 sales leaders).
8. **New roles are appearing.** Salesforce has created roles such as AI conversation designer and forward-deployed engineer. Bloomberry, which sells job-postings data, counts GTM engineering and RevOps postings together and finds them up 205% in January to September 2025 against the same months of 2024, with SQL and Python each appearing in 38% of GTM-engineer postings.

**Practices associated with leaders.** Test each in your own data:

- pick a few high-volume workflows;
- write down the best performer's process;
- measure each unit of work against a comparison group;
- keep people accountable for exceptions and money;
- reinvest freed capacity on purpose;
- build internal capability (GTM engineering, evaluation and governance) rather than collecting point tools.

**What holds laggards back.**

- Data and process debt.
- Tools layered onto unchanged workflows.
- No plan for freed capacity.
- Missing controls for agents.
- Skills, roles and incentives that have not changed.

**Where to start.** Name three to five workflows, each with a single owner. Measure the baseline and keep a comparison group. Decide in advance where freed capacity goes, and what result, over what period, would justify scaling.

## 1. Scope, sources and method

This report examines what the public record shows about AI's effect on go-to-market work, and what separates the companies reporting value from those that are not. It is written for chief revenue officers and for leaders of sales, marketing, customer success, support and revenue operations.

**Dataset and range.** The evidence base is the set of findings in Appendix A, drawn from four kinds of public material published between February 2024 and November 20, 2026. The data coverage note above explains what the Q3 2026 earnings season has not yet produced.

| Source type | Examples used here | What it can show | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Filings and earnings calls | Fiserv (November 3, 2026), HubSpot (November 4, 2026) | Results stated under disclosure rules, sometimes in financial terms | Rarely explains how the work changed; the Q3 season is still under way |
| Operator disclosures | Fin (formerly Intercom; part of Salesforce since September 2026), Klarna, Salesforce, Shopify, Vercel, Zapier | How a workflow was rebuilt, and what happened to the people | Self-reported, often by companies that sell the tools; definitions change over time |
| Independent and academic research | Gartner, BCG, McKinsey, Bain, two arXiv preprints, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* | Patterns across many organizations, one peer-reviewed causal field study and two experiments that have not been peer reviewed | Most surveys capture perception and compare groups chosen by outcome or maturity |
| Vendor and platform data | Microsoft Work Trend Index, Gong, Salesforce State of Sales, LeanData, Salesloft, Seismic, Instantly, Bloomberry, ChurnZero | Large datasets on specific GTM activities | Vendors study their own users and often sell the remedy |

**Evidence grades.** Each entry in Appendix A is graded on what it proves. The companion report on Q3 2026 earnings, still in review, uses the same grades:

- **A, financial:** the effect is stated in financial terms, such as a cost saving, a return or a profit improvement.
- **B, measured:** a quantified operating or labor-market result, such as a resolution rate, a headcount change or employment data, with no financial translation.
- **C, survey, forecast or narrative:** perceptions, predictions, comparisons between groups or qualitative claims. A forecast or a target is C whatever its units, so a savings target stated in dollars is not graded A.

Each entry also states its basis: **observed** (measured in records or accounts), **self-reported** (a company or respondent describing its own results), **estimated** (a respondent's or company's estimate) or **forecast**.

**Reading the numbers.**

- Most leader-versus-laggard gaps compare groups at one point in time, and several studies define the groups by the outcome itself, such as growth, margins or market share. They show association, not cause.
- Companies define “resolved,” “contained,” “handled” and “automated” differently, and some changed their definitions during 2026. Rates are not like-for-like, and a trend needs one cohort and one definition.
- Where a company has published several figures for the same thing, this report uses the most recent dated figure and names what it counts.
- Vendor-sponsored findings are flagged in the text and in the register.
- Payment records can be a harder measure than surveys, but only for the population they cover. Carta, for example, reports that 57.7% of the fund managers among its U.S. fund administration customers had paid Anthropic or OpenAI in the 12 months to Q3 2026. That describes fund managers' spending, not GTM teams, so it is not used as evidence here.

## 2. Adoption has outrun readiness

AI is now standard equipment for revenue teams. Readiness to run it at scale, and evidence that it pays, lag behind.

**Figure 1.** AI use against readiness, two 2026 vendor surveys

Chart, in the live doc: AI use against readiness in two 2026 vendor surveys; compare within each survey, not across them. Salesloft (500 U.S. revenue leaders at companies with 200+ employees, May–June 2026): 100% use AI somewhere in the revenue process; 20.6% call it production-ready with measurable outcomes. LeanData (157 GTM practitioners in its customer survey, May 2026): 93% have at least one AI agent deployed; readiness 31% ready, 63% somewhat ready, 5% not ready (question-level result, p. 7). Sources: Salesloft, 2026 Revenue Benchmark Report, U.S. (500 leaders, May 28–June 10, 2026); LeanData, The 2026 State of AI Go-to-Market Readiness Report (157 practitioners), p. 7

The same pattern appears where outcomes are measured, though most of the measures below are self-assessments:

- **Time saved is not time reinvested.** AI saves sellers 4.8 hours a week on average, but most sales organizations report low reinvestment of that time (Figure 5). Organizations with moderate to large time savings that reinvest them in high-impact sales work are 2.2 times as likely to exceed customer-growth goals, and 3.1 times as likely to exceed lead-to-opportunity conversion goals, as those that reinvest less. This is an association among 210 surveyed sales leaders, not a measured effect.
- **More agents, little felt productivity.** Gartner predicts agents will outnumber sellers 10 to 1 by 2028, while fewer than 40% of sellers will say agents improved their productivity. Its analyst warns that if the underlying systems are fragmented, “the agents will scale the fragmentation.”
- **Few report revenue impact.** In Highspot's 2025 survey of 463 sales and revenue leaders, 28% said AI improves revenue-driving sales performance. Among 2,002 CIOs and technology executives surveyed by Gartner in 2026, 13% report significant or transformational value from AI.
- **Seller productivity has plateaued.** In Gong's survey of 3,048 revenue leaders, leaders estimated that 46% of their reps reached quota in 2025, down from an estimated 52% in 2024. Gong's separate analysis of 7.1 million opportunities at 3,613 organizations found win rates and deal length steady in 2025 while reps worked fewer opportunities.

Access is no longer the constraint. The distance between using AI and showing results is a gap in operating model and in measurement, and the rest of this report examines who has narrowed it and how.

### Case 1. Fiserv: unit economics in a regulated call center

- **What changed:** Two AI-enabled workflows in Fiserv's turnaround: an agentic call center built with Sierra, and agents embedded in the risk-hold process at Clover, its merchant platform.
- **Call center (Q3 2026 call, November 3, 2026):** A containment rate above 40% and a 5–10x return on token costs. Management said there was no negative effect on customer NPS. It gave no count of eligible contacts and no NPS sample.
- **Clover risk holds (same call):** Decision times fell by two-thirds and false positives by more than 40% after agents were embedded. Management gave no period and no count of cases.
- **Company-wide target and costs, not measured AI results:** Project Elevate's savings target rose to $1 billion from $500 million. On the call, management attributed the increase to a portfolio review and to early results from deploying AI; the release names only the review and added efficiencies. The newly identified additional savings will come largely after 2027. Separately, Fiserv excluded $158 million in Q3 and $487 million over 9 months of One Fiserv transformation program expenses from adjusted results. Management linked the two, expecting at least a 1:1 return on Elevate savings against transformation expense ([call transcript](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/fisv/transcripts/726147-q3-2026/)), but the release does not break the costs down by program.
- **Apply it:** Report AI returns per workflow and say what the cost base includes. A return on token costs leaves out build, review, escalation and rework.

### Case 2. Salesforce: customer zero, with redeployment

- **What changed:** Salesforce runs its own agents on its help site and uses an SDR agent to work low-score leads its people could not reach. Its CEO cited a backlog of 100 million uncalled leads built up over 26 years ([TechRadar, September 2, 2025](https://www.techradar.com/pro/salesforce-says-it-cuts-4-000-support-jobs-and-replaced-them-with-ai)).
- **Results, as Salesforce reports them:** Its help agent has passed 5 million customer conversations, with 64% resolved autonomously (earnings call, August 26, 2026; period not stated). Its earlier figures were worded differently each time: a 77% resolution rate in September 2025, 63% of questions resolved on 2.6 million help-site conversations in a year (January 2026, with satisfaction roughly on par with human agents) and 70% of 4.3 million inquiries (June 25, 2026). The four may not be one measure. The SDR agent created 3,200 opportunities in 4 months (early 2026).
- **Staffing, from two sources a few months apart:** The CEO said in an interview reported on September 1, 2025 that support headcount had gone from about 9,000 to about 5,000. A [company article of January 15, 2026](https://www.salesforce.com/au/news/stories/agentic-enterprise-workforce-evolution/) says the support team shrank through attrition and redeployment and gives no headcount. It also says that hundreds of employees moved into high-growth areas, that the Evaluations team doubled by recruiting from support and that the company is adding thousands of sales and professional services roles.
- **New roles:** forward-deployed engineers, deployment strategists, AI conversation designers and AI architects.
- **Caveats:** Company figures, not independently verified, from the vendor of the product. Salesforce's fiscal Q3 results arrive after this report's cutoff.
- **Apply it:** Decide where freed capacity goes before launch, and staff the roles that keep agents good: evaluation, conversation design and deployment.

### Case 3. Klarna: two accounts of one decision

- **The 2024 forecast:** In February 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of customer service chats and the work of 700 full-time agents. It estimated a $40 million profit improvement for 2024.
- **The reported outcome for 2024:** In a July 2025 letter to SEC staff, Klarna said the assistant delivered about $39 million of cost savings in 2024 and handled 66% of chats in the 12 months to March 31, 2025. AI-handled chats were resolved in about 2 minutes, against a 12-minute average for human agents.
- **The 2025 figures:** In its annual report for 2025, Klarna says the assistant handled 80% of customer service chats in the year and saved about $59 million. It puts the assistant's work at more than 850 full-time agents and says customer satisfaction was on a par with human agents, by its own surveys.
- **The disputed 2025 change of course:** In May 2025 the CEO told Bloomberg that cost had weighed too heavily and quality had suffered, and Klarna began recruiting human agents. In its SEC letter, Klarna said the CEO's quality remarks concerned a pilot of freelance customer experience consultants, not AI service quality, and described a dual track of AI plus high-quality human support. The letter answers a Forbes article and does not name the outlet that ran the interview.
- **Apply it:** On either reading the lesson holds: set quality and customer measures beside cost from day one, and keep a route to a person.

### Case 4. Vercel: a vendor's account of building from the best performer

- **Read it as a claim:** Vercel sells the platform its agents run on. The figures below come from its own posts, an investor's write-up of an interview and coverage of a talk. None publishes a denominator, and the figures vary between retellings.
- **What changed:** A GTM engineering team builds agents for go-to-market functions. One engineer built the inbound lead agent while spending about a fifth of their time on it, and the top SDR wrote its first instructions ([write-up of October 6, 2026](https://tomtunguz.com/how-to-automate-inbound/)).
- **How:** The top performer reviewed every output at first, and review later moved to a random sample. When the lead agent's instructions grew to about 1,000 lines, deterministic rules moved into code and the model kept the judgment calls (same write-up).
- **What Vercel reports, snapshot by snapshot:** February 9, 2026: one SDR does the work of 10, and the support agent answers 87% of initial questions. June 17, 2026: the lead agent costs about $5,000 a year to run and returns 32 times that, with an engineer's part-time work described separately; the support agent solves 92% of tickets; more than 100 agents are in production. October 6, 2026, in the investor's write-up: about $1,000 a year in inference and infrastructure for the lead agent, plus a fifth of one engineer's time. October 15, 2026: the support agent automates 91% of support tickets. A later talk, as SaaStr reports it: 93% of total case volume, and a lead agent costing under $5,000 a year in infrastructure and tokens. Whether the 32 times counts the engineer's time is not stated.
- **Apply it:** Start with a workflow your best person can describe step by step, keep that person reviewing until the corrections stop and publish your own denominator.

### Case 5. Fin and HubSpot: what a resolution rate measures

- **Fin** was called Intercom until May 12, 2026, when the company took the name of its AI agent; the customer service platform kept the Intercom name. Salesforce completed its acquisition of Fin on September 10, 2026, so Fin's figures are now those of a Salesforce product. The company made itself its own reference customer: it moved its support team to an AI-first model, created a conversation designer role and reports that Fin resolves more than 81% of its own support volume (March 13, 2026). Across customers it reports a 76% average resolution rate, public by June 9, 2026. Fin then narrowed the denominator: conversations in which it could not answer no longer count, for new workspaces from June 25 and for existing ones from July 1 to 8, 2026. That can raise a reported rate with no change in outcomes, and the same 76% is still quoted after the change. Its [$0.99 fee](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-pricing-outcomes) applies to confirmed resolutions and to procedure handoffs. It also applies to “assumed” resolutions, where a customer leaves without asking for more help. A clarifying question that gets no reply is not billed, and a resolution is deducted if the customer comes back.
- **HubSpot** gives an average resolution rate for Customer Agent on its earnings calls: 55% in August 2025, in the 60s in November 2025, the mid-60s in February 2026, 70% in May 2026 and 72% of support tickets without human escalation in August 2026. In May 2026 it also said the rate had gone “from 20% last year”, with no date for the 20%. In November 2026 it gave no rate and said the agent resolved more than 2 million support issues in the quarter. HubSpot does not say which customers or tickets the rate covers. Separately, it said more than 10,000 customers had adopted Customer Agent by Q2 2026. Its CEO says customers start with internal-facing agents, such as its Data Agent.
- **Apply it:** Before comparing vendors or tracking progress, fix one cohort and one definition, sample assumed resolutions, and pay for verified completions where you can.

## 5. The negative impacts, and who bears them

The costs are real, and they fall unevenly: on customers when quality slips, on early-career workers when entry-level work is automated and on teams asked to run new tools on old processes.

### What happens to the people

**Figure 3.** Workforce actions reported by customer service leaders

Chart, in the live doc: share of 275 service and support leaders reporting each workforce action, fielded July and August 2026. Shifted agents into new roles within service and support 71%; froze hiring 45%; moved agents to other business units 39%; reduced agent headcount because of AI 21%; used forced reductions 20%; plan to rehire within 18 months 11%; rehired after earlier reductions 7%. Among those that cut headcount, the average cut was 16.7% over 18 months. Gartner's release ties only the headcount reduction to AI and does not say whether leaders could report several actions. Source: Gartner survey of 275 customer service and support leaders, fielded July and August 2026, published November 5, 2026

More service leaders report moving agents into other roles than cutting headcount. Gartner's release ties only the headcount cut to AI, and it does not say whether respondents could report several actions. Few revenue leaders expect replacement: in Apollo's 2026 survey of 328 GTM professionals, a vendor survey, 6% of sales and marketing leaders believe AI will replace members of their team. Gartner advises treating headcount reductions as the result of proven, sustained AI performance rather than a precondition for value.

The pressure lands hardest at the entry level. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab finds that employment of U.S. workers aged 22 to 25 in highly AI-exposed occupations, customer service among them, is 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with peers in less-exposed occupations. That is a relative gap in payroll data, not a count of jobs lost. The lab finds no economy-wide displacement, and the decline is concentrated where AI automates work rather than complements it.

## 6. What leaders do differently

Across independent studies, the companies reporting results share an operating model, not a tool list. The studies compare groups, and several define the groups by the outcome itself, so read the gaps as associations and test the practices in your own data.

McKinsey grouped companies by change in market share: leaders grew share by more than 10% in a year, and laggards lost more than 5%. Other studies point the same way, with the same limits:

- **BCG** finds that the 7.5% of companies it calls future-built had 2.4 times the revenue growth, 2.3 times the shareholder return and 2.8 times the EBITDA growth of laggards over 2022 to 2025, figures it takes from financial databases, and that a further 41% are scaling AI and also outperforming. Its groups are defined by AI maturity. In its 2025 study, future-built companies had deployed 62% of their AI initiatives, against 12% for laggards.
- **Bain**'s “winners,” selected for top-quartile revenue growth in their sector and region and for beating margin targets, report twice the AI-attributed revenue growth of laggards. Bain presents their redesign of work as a description of how they operate; it does not test cause.
- **Gong**, a vendor, finds in its platform records that sellers who use AI frequently generate 77% more revenue per rep than those who do not, across 7.1 million opportunities at 3,613 organizations. It also reports that organizations with AI at the core of their strategy are 65% more likely to have raised win rates than those running limited pilots, and does not say which data that figure rests on.
- **Gartner** finds that sales organizations giving sellers AI next-best actions are 2.6 times as likely to achieve commercial growth, and that those prioritizing upskilling sellers on AI are 2.4 times as likely to report strong revenue growth. Its release ties a survey of 227 CSOs, fielded August to September 2025, to the upskilling figure and names no sample for the first.

None of these proves that AI caused the difference; better-run companies may simply adopt AI faster. The practices behind the gaps are consistent, though, and each can be tested.

## 7. What holds the laggards back

The barriers leaders name are mostly operational. Few surveys find the models themselves to be the binding constraint; most point to what surrounds them.

Seven patterns recur in the laggards' record:

1. **Tools layered on unchanged workflows.** Gartner warns that agents placed on fragmented systems multiply the fragmentation, and predicts that by 2028 chief sales officers who overhaul data, automation and user experience will be 5 times as likely to gain a return on AI as those who choose quick fixes.
2. **Data and process debt.** Bad data, undocumented processes and siloed teams are the root causes GTM practitioners named in LeanData's survey when AI underperforms. Agents inherit every gap in the CRM and every step nobody wrote down.
3. **No plan for freed capacity.** Without a reinvestment plan, saved hours are absorbed by lower-value work, and the productivity case never reaches the P&L.
4. **Measuring activity instead of outcomes.** Only 31% of CIOs have rules for stopping an AI pilot before production, and 17% have rules for retiring low-value AI that is already running (Gartner).
5. **Cost as the only objective.** Klarna's experience, on either reading (Case 3), and Gartner's warning against cutting fast and rehiring later show the risk when savings lead and quality follows.
6. **Missing controls.** In BCG's survey, 5% of companies have all six of its controls for agents in place (Table 2).
7. **Unchanged roles, skills and incentives.** Only 4% of CIOs report explicit plans to redesign roles for a human-agent workforce (Gartner), and even among knowledge workers who already use AI, Microsoft classes just 16% as Frontier Professionals, its most advanced users.

## 8. A 90-day playbook

The leaders' sequence can be compressed into one quarter for a first set of workflows. Each phase ends at a gate, and the next phase starts only when the gate is passed. Day 90 supports a decision to scale, hold or stop a workflow. A hold is a valid result: it means the evidence is not in yet. Outcomes that take longer to show, such as renewals, need a longer observation window.

**Figure 6.** A 90-day path from chosen workflow to a scale decision

Diagram, in the live doc: 90 days, three phases and a gate before each step up; a phase starts only when the gate before it is passed. Phase 1, Choose and baseline (days 1 to 30): pick 3–5 high-volume workflows, name one owner for each, write down the best process, baseline cost and quality, plan where freed time goes. Gate 1, baseline set: comparison group in place. Phase 2, Build and shadow (days 31 to 60): build with the top performer, review every output, log each correction, move fixed rules into code, add cost caps and rollback. Gate 2, decision rule met: predeclared quality margin held over the minimum volume. Phase 3, Scale and redeploy (days 61 to 90): step review down to samples, widen to the whole workflow, redeploy capacity as planned, reset quotas and comp plans, report verified unit costs. Throughout: AI fluency in every role, a GTM engineer, clean and connected data, and an owner and controls for every agent. Analyst synthesis of the practices in sections 4 to 7.

**Table 4.** Measurement protocol for each workflow

| Element | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost basis | Total cost of all assigned cases, including agent run costs, implementation, review, escalation and rework, divided by verified completions; the same accounting for the comparison group | A vendor fee or a return on token costs is not a fully loaded cost |
| Volume funnel | Eligible, attempted, handed-off and verified-complete counts, every week | A narrower denominator can raise a reported rate with no change in outcomes |
| Quality | Satisfaction, accuracy or conversion against the human baseline, plus a separate sample of silent exits and assumed resolutions | Silent exits can be counted as success and billed as outcomes |
| Review | Review coverage, the share of work a person checks, reported separately from overrides among reviewed cases | Fewer reviews can make the override rate look better |
| Commercial impact | Hours released, hours redeployed and cash savings, recorded separately; incremental conversion, gross profit or retention against the comparison group, kept as coverage expands | Shows whether freed capacity produced growth |
| Decision rule | Declared before launch: a quality margin against the baseline, critical-error rules, a minimum volume and an observation window | Keeps the scale decision from being argued after the fact |

**Decisions to make in week one**

1. **Which workflows.** Three to five, each with a unit of work (a lead, a ticket, a renewal) and a single owner.
2. **What counts as success.** The cost basis, quality margin and comparison group in Table 4, set before launch rather than read from before-and-after anecdotes.
3. **Where freed capacity goes.** Named in advance: outbound, expansion, strategic accounts or slower backfill.
4. **Who builds.** A GTM engineer or builder, paired with the function's best performer.
5. **What would stop an agent.** Critical-error rules, cost caps and a rollback path, agreed before launch.

**Tests to run in customer success.** Public evidence is thin here, so run these in your own data: whether agent-flagged renewal risk shows up earlier than your team's own signals; whether value reviews can be prepared faster with no loss of accuracy; whether digital-only coverage loses relationship signal.

## 10. Outlook: November 2026 to November 2028

Five shifts look likely over the next 24 months, each grounded in a forecast or a trend already under way. Forecasts for 2030 fall outside this window and are listed separately below.

1. **Agents multiply, and our read is that value moves to orchestration.** Gartner expects agents to outnumber sellers 10 to 1 by 2028, while fewer than 40% of sellers say agents improved their productivity. On that forecast the constraint becomes coordination, data and governance, not access to agents. This is a read and no study has tested it, and two early experiments cut against it. In a randomized trial on 100 websites, a single sales agent raised revenue per visitor by 10.0%. In a controlled comparison across 13 enterprise-style procedures, coordinated agent designs used 1.4 to 4.7 times the tokens of a single agent with no overall gain in success. Both are preprints that have not been peer reviewed, and neither tests live GTM work across a revenue process.
2. **Buyers arrive briefed by AI.** 45% of B2B buyers already report using GenAI in a recent purchase, and 69% prefer to validate AI-generated insights with a sales rep (Gartner). McKinsey finds gen AI now ranks among the top five channels buyers use to find and evaluate suppliers. Sellers' value shifts toward validation, quantification and confidence. On the seller side, Gartner predicts that 95% of sellers' research workflows will begin with AI by 2027.
3. **Service stays hybrid.** Gartner predicts none of the Fortune 500 will have fully eliminated human customer service by 2028. Expect fewer, more skilled people working the complex cases.
4. **Entry-level roles get redesigned.** Stanford's lab now updates its young-worker measures monthly, so the 19% gap will show whether entry-level pressure is spreading. GTM engineering is the first revenue role built around agent work, and entry roles will need redesigning around reviewing and supervising AI.
5. **Pricing follows the work.** Per-resolution and usage pricing are spreading, led by Fin and Salesforce, which now owns it. Revenue teams will increasingly sell, forecast and renew in units of work done, and the definition of a billable outcome becomes a negotiating point.

**Longer-range forecasts, outside this window.** BCG projects that agentic AI will account for about 40% of AI value by 2030, up from 22% in 2026 and 17% in 2025, and that dedicated AI roles will grow from 7% of the workforce in 2026 to 22% by 2030. Companies in its survey expect workforce reductions of 10–15% by 2030, with middle management and frontline leadership the most exposed layers. These are forecasts, not observations.

**Five questions for the next leadership offsite**

1. Which three workflows would we rebuild first, and who owns each?
2. What does a verified unit of work cost us today, by person and by agent?
3. Where will freed capacity go, and how will we know it got there?
4. How will new sellers and CSMs learn the craft if AI does the entry-level work?
5. What result, declared in advance, would make us scale, hold or stop an agent?

## Appendix B: Sources and caveats

**Caveats.** Check these before reusing any figure.

- **Coverage.** Evidence runs through November 23, 2026. The narrative in sections 1 to 10 is the edition of November 20, 2026. Records added since are in Appendix A. The Q3 2026 earnings season is incomplete, and companies with off-calendar quarters, including Salesforce, report after the cutoff.
- **Who compiled and reviewed it.** Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, compiled the findings and checked each against the source it links to. Nathan Fierley edited the report and decided what went in. On November 20, 2026 a second AI model, GPT-6.2 Astra, made by OpenAI, rechecked the 54 findings then in the register and disputed 23. Of those, 22 were confirmed and corrected that day, and the last, Carta's, on November 24, 2026, once a person had opened its page. Later that day 11 records were added: five from ICONIQ, four from Deloitte, one from Citi and one from HubSpot. The Fiserv row on Project Elevate was also corrected a second time, after a reader pointed to the reasons management gave. None of these was part of that recheck. On November 22, 2026, 12 more records were added and three rows corrected, each after two source checks by Claude and outside that recheck. Five of the 12 are from BCG and two are on SAP, and those seven reached the register as leads from the field. The other five are from earnings calls. The three corrections replace or date figures from Salesforce and HubSpot. On November 23, 2026, eight more records were added, each after two source checks by Claude and outside that recheck. Six came from a weekly scan of new publications and two from a lookback over 18 months of earnings calls. The same day a model from another lab checked the eight against their sources. It disputed no figure. Five rows were reworded and three links were replaced with the publishers' own releases, as Appendix C lists. On November 25, 2026, five more records were added, each after two source checks by Claude and outside that recheck. All five reached the register as leads from the field: one from Monaco and four from Anthropic. The report has not been peer reviewed, and no outside expert has reviewed it.
- **Vendor sources.** Many sources are vendors reporting on their own products, their customers or their own platform's data: Vercel, Salesforce, Fin (part of Salesforce since September 10, 2026), HubSpot, ServiceNow, Microsoft, Gong, LeanData, Salesloft, Instantly, Seismic, Highspot, Apollo, Bloomberry, ChurnZero, Richardson, SnapLogic, Zendesk, Monaco and Anthropic. Read their figures as claims.
- **Definitions.** Resolution, containment, handling and automation are defined differently by each company, and Fin narrowed its resolution-rate denominator between June 25 and July 8, 2026. The rates in Figure 2 are not like-for-like.
- **Associations.** Leader-versus-laggard comparisons (McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Gong, Gartner) compare groups at one point in time, and several define the groups by outcome. They do not show that AI caused the difference.
- **Causal evidence.** The *QJE* field study is causal for support agents at one company; it does not automatically carry over to other functions. Two arXiv preprints report experiments: a randomized trial of one sales agent on websites, and a controlled comparison of agent designs on enterprise-style procedures. Neither has been peer reviewed, and neither tests live GTM work across a revenue process.
- **Vercel.** Figures come from the company's posts, an investor's write-up of an interview and coverage of a talk, without published denominators, and they vary between retellings. Case 4 lists each dated figure with what it counts.
- **Klarna.** The 2024 profit figure was a forecast. The reported 2024 savings come from Klarna's letter to SEC staff, and the 2025 figures from its annual report. The 2025 change of course has two accounts (Case 3).
- **Fiserv.** Call-center results, Clover risk-hold results, Project Elevate savings and One Fiserv transformation expenses have different scopes and are kept separate. The release gives a portfolio review as the reason for the higher Elevate target, and the call adds early results from deploying AI. One transcript marks the word before “rate above 40%” as inaudible, and a second reads “a containment rate above 40%”.
- **BCG.** The full report says its financial multiples come from Capital IQ and Orbis for 2022 to 2025. It was read in a copy hosted on another site. BCG's 2025 edition, on BCG's own site, says the same of its figures.
- **Gartner.** Four releases, dated May 11, July 28, September 2 and October 19, 2026, would not open for the checker and were read through search results that show their text.
- **Deloitte.** The survey covers only companies that already have AI in daily use, so its shares are not shares of all companies. Its multi-agent figures rest on the 2,372 companies that run several agents in one workflow. Deloitte advises companies on AI.
- **Citi.** The figure counts Citi's own engineers, not GTM staff, and the call does not give their total. It is in the register as an adoption figure from the earnings record.
- **ICONIQ.** An investor's survey of executives at software companies that build AI products, some of them its own portfolio companies. Its shares describe that group and not companies in general. Several of its figures are executives' estimates, and the shares for headcount plans are sums of the bands the report prints.
- **BCG's survey of revenue leaders.** A survey of 398 revenue leaders, published October 19, 2026, with no fieldwork dates. Its chart labels the impact figures as the share ranking each area first or second, while its text words them as shares that report an improvement. The gaps it reports for organizations with more autonomous agents are associations, and it gives no threshold or size for that group.
- **SAP.** The figures come from an article in HBR by two Harvard Business School authors (March 27, 2025), which does not say how the authors obtained them. No SAP document was opened. The shorter sales cycle compares the Digital Hubs with SAP's in-person selling to large customers, so it is not a like-for-like test of AI.
- **Earnings calls in third-party transcripts.** The rows added or corrected on November 22, 2026 from Salesforce, HubSpot, ServiceNow, FactSet, JPMorgan, HCLTech and Infosys were read in transcripts published by other sites, not in company documents. So were the rows added on November 23, 2026 from Lowe's and Walmart.
- **Lowe's and Walmart.** Both are consumer retailers, outside B2B selling. Each compares customers who use an AI shopping assistant with customers who do not. Neither call describes an assignment or an adjustment, or gives a sample, a period or a method, so the gap is an association between two groups and not a measured lift.
- **Fin on Salesforce's help portal.** Fin's page gives no publication date and no count or period for the 62%. Salesforce's post of November 9, 2026 gives the same 62% for the first few weeks after launch. Neither says how Fin relates to the help agent whose 64% is in Figure 2. Fin's page says Fin was to handle every conversation on the English-language portal from November 18, and does not say that it has.
- **Microsoft's internal study.** The post gives no number of sellers or deals, no period and no comparison group, and it does not say which product was measured. Its footnote cites internal telemetry from a controlled environment and CRM data.
- **Richardson.** Richardson sells sales training. Its two groups are defined by quota attainment, which respondents report themselves, and its release calls the comparisons correlational. The two groups hold 835 of the 1,105 leaders, and the other 270 are in neither. The 70% and the 4% are in the release and not on the report page. The full report sits behind a form and was not opened.
- **SnapLogic.** The report does not define an abandoned project or give a period for it. The body of its release says AI projects where the headline and the report say agentic AI projects, and the release gives 64% for moderate or significant overspend where the report's shares add to 63%. The 89% is of the respondents who answered the question on overspend, and counts slight overspend with moderate and significant.
- **Gartner's survey on the CMO role.** The figures are expectations. The release describes the 282 respondents as senior executive leaders and does not say whether all are C-level, whether CMOs were among them or which countries were covered.
- **Zendesk.** Two surveys in 10 countries, one of business respondents and one of consumers, weighted by Zendesk, with no fieldwork firm named. Business leaders are 74% of the business respondents and human agents 26%. Zendesk sells customer service software.
- **Monaco.** The figures are the chief executive's own account, in a podcast interview, of how his company uses the platform it sells. He defines no outbound opportunity and gives no reply, meeting or conversion rate and no baseline. The months are not dated, and the 500 is a pace for a month still under way. He tells customers not to expect the same, because the outreach goes out in his name.
- **Anthropic's interview study.** Anthropic makes Claude, the model that compiled this register. The 58 companies are Anthropic's own enterprise customers, chosen for range and not as a representative sample. The interview dates are not given and value is as interviewees described it. An automated reviewer, whose model is not named, coded the applications. Three of the four rows are single cases from unnamed companies, with no figures.
- **Forecasts.** Gartner's 2027 and 2028 figures and BCG's 2030 figures are predictions. Stanford's findings are descriptive, not causal.
- **Carta.** Cited as a methods example only. Its 57.7% covers the management companies of Carta's U.S.-based fund administration customers, most of them VC firms, and counts payments to Anthropic or OpenAI in the 12 months to Q3 2026. The row was corrected on November 24, 2026, after a second reader reported a narrower population than it gave.

**Unresolved points**

1. How many of the 3,800 respondents answered the question behind Salesforce's estimate that 19% of company data is siloed, inaccessible or otherwise unusable.
2. Whether the 76% that Fin still quotes was computed on its old denominator or its new one.
3. Whether Vercel's 32x return counts the engineer's time or only the agent's running cost.
4. How Fiserv's excluded transformation expenses divide between Project Elevate and other programs.
