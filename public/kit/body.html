Our read: agents are multiplying faster than teams can coordinate them

This is our read of the evidence, and no public study has tested it yet. Nearly every team surveyed already uses AI, and Gartner expects agents to outnumber sellers 10 to 1 by 2028. The clearest reported gains come from companies that rebuilt a workflow around AI, measured it by the unit of work and redeployed the time it freed. We expect that doing this at scale takes two disciplines.

Agent enablement

Getting an agent ready to do a job well: a charter, the right context, instructions written from the best performer’s process, an evaluation set, a scorecard and review until the corrections stop. It is the lifecycle you already run for a new hire.

The enablement play

Orchestration

Coordinating many agents and people across the revenue process: one shared record, designed handoffs, approvals and escalation, a log of every step and unit costs measured across the whole workflow.

The orchestration play

What would change our read

Public evidence on orchestration is still thin. Most measured results come from single agents in single workflows, and most leader-versus-laggard gaps are associations.

Two preprints from autumn 2026 already cut against it, and neither has been peer reviewed. In a randomized trial on 100 websites, a single sales agent raised revenue per visitor by 10.0%, so one agent can produce a measured gain with no orchestration at all. In a controlled comparison across 13 enterprise-style procedures, coordinated agent designs used 1.4 to 4.7 times the tokens of a single agent with no overall gain in success. Neither tests live GTM work across a revenue process.

We will review this read by May 20, 2027, which is 6 months from this edition. We will review it sooner if a study of live GTM work appears. We will drop it if studies with a comparison group show coordinated agents costing more per verified completion than a single agent at the same quality, in lead handling, support escalation or renewal preparation. We will narrow it if single agents alone keep producing measured gains, as the website trial did. Evidence either way goes in the register.

AI and the Redefinition of GTM Work: Leaders, Laggards and the Evidence

Research report, revised November 20, 2026. Reviewed by its editor and by AI models; not peer reviewed.

AI is redefining go-to-market work, but the evidence of results is concentrated. The clearest reported gains come from companies that rebuilt a specific workflow around AI, measured it by the unit of work and redeployed the time it freed. Beyond those cases the record is mixed, not negative.

Read the reportSee what the revision changed

Eight findings

  1. Almost every team uses AI; a minority say it is ready.
  2. Where work has been rebuilt, companies report automation rates from above 30% to 91%, each on its own measure.
  3. Leaders and laggards differ sharply, but the comparisons are associations.
  4. The leaders’ common practice is redesign before automation.
  5. More service leaders report moving people than cutting them, and entry-level employment is weakening.
  6. Cutting on cost alone risks quality, though the best-known case is disputed.
  7. Most sales organizations report low reinvestment of the time AI saves.
  8. New roles are appearing.

Orchestration keeps people and agents working from one record

Gartner expects agents to outnumber sellers 10 to 1 by 2028. Its analyst puts the risk plainly: on fragmented systems, “the agents will scale the fragmentation.”

  1. PeopleOwners, sellers, customer success and reviewers: judgment, exceptions, relationships and money
  2. AgentsOne per unit of work: lead, research, support, renewal preparation
  3. OrchestrationRouting, handoffs, approvals, escalation and a log of every step
  4. Shared contextAccounts, commitments, history and permissions that every agent and person reads from
  5. Systems of recordCRM, billing, support desk and product usage, kept as they are
  • Keep the systems of record.Add only what none of them holds: the shared context and the rules.
  • Stay vendor-neutral.Models and agents will change; your context, rules and measures should not have to.
  • Let every handoff carry the promise.What was committed to the customer travels with the work, so the next agent or person starts from it.
  • Measure the whole workflow.Cost per verified completion from end to end, across every agent and person involved.

Check your maturity

Ninety days to a scale, hold or stop decision

One quarter is enough to decide on a first set of workflows. Each phase ends at a gate, and the next starts only when the evidence passes it. A hold at day 90 is a valid result: it means the evidence is not in yet. Renewals and other slow outcomes need a longer window.

Decisions to make in week one

  1. Which workflows. Three to five, each with a unit of work (a lead, a ticket, a renewal) and a single owner.
  2. What counts as success. The cost basis, quality margin and comparison group, set before launch.
  3. Where freed capacity goes. Named in advance: outbound, expansion, strategic accounts or slower backfill.
  4. Who builds. A GTM engineer or builder, paired with the function’s best performer.
  5. What would stop an agent. Critical-error rules, cost caps and a rollback path, agreed before launch.

How the evidence is graded

   

    
    
     
  • A FinancialThe effect is stated in financial terms, such as a cost saving, a return or a profit improvement, and it has been reported, not forecast.
    • 
     
  • B MeasuredA quantified operating or labor-market result, with no financial translation.
    • 
     
  • C Survey, forecast or narrativePerceptions, predictions, comparisons between groups or qualitative claims.
    • 
    

    
Basis. Each finding is also marked as observed in records or accounts, self-reported by a company or respondent, estimated, or forecast.

    
Rules. One dated figure per claim, with what it counts. A forecast or a target is graded C whatever its units. Leader-versus-laggard gaps are labeled as associations. A vendor is a source that sells the product it measures, reports from its own platform’s data or surveys the market it sells to, and vendors are flagged. A company reporting on its own operations is not a vendor. Example values in the tools are marked as examples.
